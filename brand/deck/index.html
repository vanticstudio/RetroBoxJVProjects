Retro Box JV Projects

The cable box you fell asleep in front of.

A spare mini PC, a folder of your own video, and any remote from the drawer. It comes up as a television.

JV Projects · 2026 · MIT licensed · v1.0.3

CH 02The problem

You sat down to watch something.

Ten minutes later you are still choosing.

Four apps. Three profiles. A wall of things you have already seen, a trailer that starts playing on its own, and a countdown to something you did not ask for. Every one of those screens exists to be browsed, and browsing is now the activity.

The television used to be the easiest object in the house. You pressed a button and it was on. Whatever was on was on.

Nobody set out to make watching television into work. It happened because every screen in the chain had something to recommend.

CH 03What it is

Press channel-up. Something is already playing.

A few seconds in, like you just tuned in.

Retro Box plays folders of video off a drive as if they were real TV channels. When an episode ends the next one rolls, on an endless shuffle. Channels change with the clock. A sleep timer turns the whole thing off when you don’t.

There is nothing to choose, because there is nothing to choose from.

  • No menus
  • No apps
  • No accounts
  • No recommendations
  • No algorithm
  • No internet

CH 04Why it feels like cable

Five things, and none of them are features.

  • Dayparting

    Wall-clock windows rename a channel, swap its folder, or sign it off to colour bars. 22:00 arrives and the channel changes under you, mid-episode. The schedule doesn’t wait for you to be ready.

  • Broadcast mode

    Each channel advances against the clock whether anyone is watching or not. Flip away and back ten minutes later and you have missed ten minutes.

  • Station bumpers

    A folder of idents plays between episodes, shuffled so you never get the same one twice, rolling straight into the next show. The gaps are the part you actually remember.

  • The channel guide

    One button, a green grid, and it clears itself off the screen after eight seconds without being asked. It is the only menu in the product.

  • The sleep timer

    30 → 60 → 90 → off, counting down in the corner. When it runs out it blanks the screen, or shuts the machine down properly. This is a box that lives next to a bed, and it knows it.

CH 05What it runs on

Nothing exotic. Probably nothing new.

Hardware required
A machineAny x86 mini PC that boots Linux, or a Raspberry Pi 4. 4 GB of RAM is plenty.
A displayA TV, ideally. Any HDMI monitor works.
A FlircTeaches itself any IR remote and hands it to Linux as a keyboard.
A remoteWhatever is in the drawer. Six buttons cover the basics.

Cheap hardware is the point, not a compromise.

A decade-old CPU is fine once hardware decode is on, and the installer works out which decode path the machine needs on its own — VA-API on a PC, V4L2 on a Pi. It boots straight to the picture with no login and no desktop.

An ex-office desktop off eBay is the intended machine, not the fallback.

CH 06How you get one

Take the code, or take the Saturday back.

Build your own

MIT · free · an evening

  • The whole thing is in the repo. No paid tier, no key, nothing held back.
  • One install script. It detects the platform and installs the right driver.
  • Asks you to be comfortable with a terminal and an SSH session.

Buy one ready to go

Not live yet
  • Hardware sourced and tested on a unit that is known to work.
  • OS, drivers, audio, autostart and remote already configured.
  • Plugs in and works. Someone to ask when it doesn’t.

Either way it ships empty. You supply your own files — that part does not change, and it is not something a paid box can do for you.
